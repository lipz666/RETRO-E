BASIC ORGANIC CHEMISTRY NOTES

1. Carbon atoms possessing sp3 hybridization adopt a tetrahedral geometry with bond angles near 109.5 degrees, sp2 hybridization yields a trigonal planar geometry with 120-degree angles, and sp hybridization confers a linear geometry with 180-degree angles.

2. Polar covalent bonds arise from Pauling electronegativity differences between bonded atoms, generating molecular dipole moments that elevate intermolecular attractive forces, boiling points, and aqueous solubilities relative to nonpolar analogs.

3. Brønsted-Lowry acidity increases as the conjugate base is stabilized via resonance delocalization, higher electronegativity of the charge-bearing atom, greater s-character of the hybrid orbital, or electron-withdrawing inductive effects, yielding lower pKa values.

4. Saturated hydrocarbons rely exclusively on London dispersion forces for cohesion, resulting in boiling points that scale with molecular surface area and weight but decrease systematically with constitutional chain branching.

5. Cycloalkanes experience ring strain composed of angle strain from non-ideal orbital overlap and torsional strain from eclipsing interactions; six-membered carbocycles adopt chair conformations to minimize both strain components.

6. Enantiomers are nonsuperimposable mirror-image stereoisomers possessing identical properties in achiral environments, but they rotate plane-polarized light in equal and opposite directions and exhibit distinct behaviors in chiral environments.

7. Nucleophiles are Lewis bases possessing nonbonding electron pairs or pi bonds that donate electron density to electrophiles, which are electron-deficient Lewis acids containing unoccupied orbitals or partial positive charges.

8. Bimolecular nucleophilic substitution proceeds via a concerted backside attack resulting in stereochemical inversion, whereas unimolecular substitution involves rate-determining carbocation formation accompanied by rearrangements and racemization.

9. Bimolecular elimination occurs through a concerted anti-periplanar transition state requiring an accessible beta-hydrogen, while unimolecular elimination proceeds stepwise through a carbocation intermediate, favoring the more substituted, stable alkene regioisomer.

10. Carbocation intermediates are stabilized through hyperconjugation with adjacent C–H sigma bonds, electron donation from heteroatom lone pairs, and resonance delocalization, establishing a stability hierarchy of tertiary > secondary > primary.

11. Electrophilic addition across carbon-carbon double bonds follows Markovnikov regioselectivity, wherein the electrophilic component adds to the less substituted carbon to generate the more stable carbocationic or bridged onium intermediate.

12. Aromatic systems are monocyclic, planar, completely conjugated polyenes containing (4n + 2) pi electrons that exhibit exceptional thermodynamic stability, preferring electrophilic aromatic substitution over addition to preserve ring aromaticity.

13. Carbonyl carbons exhibit electrophilic character due to polarization of the carbon-oxygen double bond toward oxygen, rendering the carbonyl carbon susceptible to nucleophilic addition or addition-elimination mechanisms.

14. Infrared spectroscopy detects functional groups by measuring vibrational absorption frequencies of covalent bonds, where carbonyl stretching appears between 1650 and 1780 cm⁻¹ and hydroxyl stretching produces broad signals between 3200 and 3600 cm⁻¹.

15. Proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy characterizes environments via chemical shifts, determines relative hydrogen quantities through integration, and reveals spin-spin coupling interactions governed by the n+1 multiplicity rule.

16. Normal-phase chromatography distributes analytes between a polar stationary phase and a nonpolar mobile phase based on polarity, causing weakly interacting nonpolar compounds to elute rapidly while strongly adsorbed polar compounds exhibit prolonged retention times.
